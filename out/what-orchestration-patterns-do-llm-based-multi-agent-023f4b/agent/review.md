# Orchestration Patterns in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems: Control, Cost, Robustness, and Evaluation

> **Question:** What orchestration patterns do LLM-based multi-agent systems use, and how do they trade off control, cost and robustness?

## Introduction

Multi-agent systems built on large language models (LLMs) coordinate several model-driven agents to tackle problems that a single model handles poorly. Much of the design effort lies in orchestration: deciding which agents act, in what order, how they communicate, and how their outputs are combined. This review asks how orchestration patterns differ in control, cost, robustness, and evaluation, and it draws only on the papers available in the corpus. The evidence is uneven. Some claims are conceptual, some come from survey discussions, and others come from individual frameworks reporting their own results. Where direct comparisons are missing, the review says so rather than inferring a ranking. The sections that follow cover pattern definitions, the centralized-versus-decentralized control question, cost trade-offs, failure propagation, and the benchmarks used to compare patterns.

## Orchestration Patterns and Their Definitions

The main axis separating orchestration patterns is where control sits. In decentralized multi-agent systems, control and decision-making are spread across agents rather than held by one coordinator [1]. Centralized designs instead place a coordinator in charge: a learned orchestrator can choose which agents to activate at each step, based on the current task state [2]. Several other patterns appear in the literature. Early LLM work relied on role-specialized pipelines, in which agents have fixed responsibilities and exchange messages through predefined protocols [3]. Debate is a related pattern in which two agents argue opposing positions while a judge agent monitors the exchange and reaches a decision [4]. Later work moved toward dynamic and self-organizing topologies, where the interaction graph is learned, searched, or adapted online [3]. Some recent frameworks make the structure explicit: coordination is modeled as a directed graph whose edges specify admissible interactions, together with an ordered orchestration process over that graph [5]. These definitions matter because the patterns differ in what is fixed in advance and what is decided at runtime, which in turn shapes the control, cost, and robustness discussed below.

## Control: Centralized Versus Decentralized Orchestration

The strongest argument for centralization in the corpus concerns coordination overhead. When each agent autonomously chooses its collaborators, the coordination cost grows, and the approach scales poorly as agents are added or changed [2]. A centralized orchestrator avoids this by separating the decision about which agent to activate from the agent's internal reasoning. In one framework, a learnable orchestrator routes agents at every step according to the current context, treating routing as a sequential decision problem [6]. Decentralized systems, by contrast, let each agent act on local information and limited messages from neighbors, which requires more sophisticated interaction algorithms to reach coherent outcomes [1]. Control can also be shared in a structured way. In one agent-network benchmark, the agents first elect a single leader, who then guides the task [7]. This is a hybrid of the two extremes: decentralized election followed by centralized execution. The corpus does not contain a direct empirical comparison of how tightly each pattern constrains agent behavior, routing, and final decisions. The control claims come mainly from conceptual discussions and from the authors of individual frameworks, so any ranking of control granularity should be treated as provisional.

## Cost Trade-offs

Cost is the second major axis, and the corpus describes it mainly through qualitative claims about debate and quantitative claims about routing. Debate is reported to be expensive: it requires multiple model calls, and long debates raise computational cost [4][4]. The same survey notes that LLMs struggle to remain coherent over long exchanges, so the extra cost does not necessarily buy better reasoning [4]. Adaptive orchestration is presented as a remedy. One difficulty-aware method reports an inference cost of $0.27 per query, against $1.66 for AFlow, and attributes the saving to avoiding redundant agent participation and adapting the workflow to easy queries [8]. The same paper criticizes MasRouter for having LLMs participate redundantly in each collaborative step and for spending heavily on easy queries without a matching gain in accuracy [8]. A chat-orchestration system, MARCO, reports a 44.91% latency reduction and a 33.71% cost reduction relative to its baselines [9]. These numbers cannot be compared directly, because the papers use different units (dollars, latency, accuracy), different tasks, and different baselines. The corpus therefore identifies the drivers of cost, namely the number of rounds, the number of participating agents, and redundant participation, but it does not support a controlled ranking of patterns at matched budgets.

## Robustness and Error Propagation

Robustness is discussed mainly as a risk that multi-agent designs can amplify. A hallucination produced by one agent can be taken up and reinforced by others, turning a small error into a cascading one [10]. The survey attributes this amplification to two factors: LLM overconfidence, in which models keep asserting outputs that are wrong, and misunderstandings between agents during collaboration [11]. Adversarial exposure is a further concern. Compromised agents can be steered into harmful behavior, and the risk grows as the number of agents increases [11]. Against these risks, the corpus reports several mitigations. In MARCO, reflection prompts reduce the recurrence of guardrail errors across retries, and most issues are resolved on the first retry [9]. A dedicated verifier agent has been proposed to check a main agent's answers, detect hallucinations, and correct them [12]. Distributed designs also offer a reliability argument: if an agent fails, its task can be reassigned to another agent [13]. None of these claims is a controlled comparison across patterns. The corpus does not show how centralized, decentralized, or debate-based designs behave under injected faults or adversarial agents, so the relative robustness of the patterns remains an open question.

## Evaluation Setups and Limitations

The benchmarks in the corpus are designed to test coordination directly rather than to rank whole systems on accuracy alone. AgentsNet builds its tasks on classic distributed-computing problems, such as leader election, to test whether agent networks can coordinate and collaborate [14][7]. Its initial suite scales to 100 agents, whereas existing benchmarks are limited to about 2 to 5 agents [14]. Its test topologies include small-world, scale-free, and Delaunay graphs ranging from 4 to 16 nodes [15]. ForestBench takes a different approach. It maps the execution traces of heterogeneous multi-agent systems into a shared graph representation, so that methods are compared on the same structural criteria, and it releases 844 queries with reference graphs of verified-successful collaborations [3]. Its results show a narrow accuracy range of 0.038 across methods, while the structural differences are wider, and the authors use this contrast to argue that accuracy alone hides how systems collaborate [16]. A third paper frames the design space as a choice between structures fixed in advance, which limit fine-grained control, and dynamic orchestration, which leaves the coordination structure implicit and unstable [17]. The corpus papers do not report matched-budget comparisons between patterns, and the cost and robustness evidence comes from different frameworks and task suites. As a result, the benchmarks indicate what should be measured, but they do not yet settle which pattern performs best.

## Conclusion

Across the corpus, orchestration choices trade control, cost, and resilience against one another, but the trade-offs are clearer in principle than in measurement. Centralized orchestration reduces the coordination overhead that arises when each agent chooses its own collaborators, and it allows routing decisions to be learned and adapted per step [2][6]. The price is a single point of control, and the corpus does not measure how this affects robustness. Debate and large, unconstrained topologies raise cost through repeated model calls and long exchanges, while adaptive routing can reduce spending on easy queries [4][8]. Error propagation is a recognized risk across patterns: hallucinations can cascade, and the risk grows with agent count [10][11]. Mitigations such as reflection guardrails and verifier agents are promising but have been shown only in individual frameworks [9][12]. Future work should compare patterns under matched compute budgets and with injected faults, reporting both accuracy and structural measures of collaboration as the benchmark work suggests [3][16]. Until such comparisons exist, practitioners should choose a pattern by matching its cost and control profile to the task, rather than by assuming one pattern is universally superior.
